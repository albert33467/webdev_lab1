Course Description

This course examines the Gothic tradition in literature from the late eighteenth century through the present. We will consider how writers use haunted spaces, secrecy, suspense, monsters, and the supernatural to explore cultural anxieties about identity, class, gender, science, and social change. Like the nightmare that Horace Walpole had at Strawberry Hill House (depicted above) that inspired The Castle of Otronto, Gothic works both terrify the soul and inspire the imagination. We'll do our best this semester to cover a wide range of source material and interpretations.

Readings will include novels, short stories, poetry, and critical essays. We will also consider selected film adaptations and discuss how visual media transform Gothic conventions.

Learning Objectives

By the end of the course, students should be able to:

  • Identify major conventions and historical developments of Gothic literature.
  • Analyze how literary texts represent cultural fears and social tensions.
  • Use appropriate literary terminology when discussing fiction, poetry, and film.
  • Construct an evidence-based interpretation of a literary text.
  • Compare how different media adapt and transform Gothic conventions.

Assignments

Reading Responses (15%)

Several times during the semester, you will write a brief response to a reading. Responses should identify a significant passage or idea and explain why it matters to your interpretation of the work.

Close Reading Essay (20%)

You will write a 4‑5 page analysis of a passage from one of the primary texts. The essay should develop a focused interpretation supported by specific textual evidence.

Film Analysis (20%)

You will compare a literary work with one of its film adaptations, paying particular attention to characterization, setting, visual composition, and changes in narrative emphasis.

Research Project (30%)

Your final project will develop an original argument about a Gothic text or cultural tradition. The project must incorporate at least three scholarly sources and will include a short presentation to the class.

Participation (15%)

Regular preparation and participation are important parts of the course. Come to class prepared to discuss the assigned readings and contribute to collaborative activities.
